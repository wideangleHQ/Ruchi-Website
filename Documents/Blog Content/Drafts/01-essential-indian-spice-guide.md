## Article body

If you open an Indian kitchen cupboard, you will rarely find dozens of exotic jars. Most everyday cooking relies on a small group of spices used in different combinations — and once you know what each one does, a recipe stops being a list to memorise and becomes something you can adjust by taste.

This guide covers the spices you will meet most often, what they taste like, when they usually go into the pan, and how to put together a simple starter pantry.

### What counts as a spice?

In everyday cooking, "spice" covers the dried seeds, bark, buds, roots and fruits that add flavour, aroma or colour: cumin seeds, cinnamon bark, cloves, turmeric root, dried chillies. Herbs (fresh or dried leaves) are usually grouped separately, although dried leaves such as bay leaf and kasoori methi (dried fenugreek leaves) often live in the same cupboard.

Spices are sold in two basic forms:

- **Whole spices** — seeds, sticks, pods or buds, usually added to hot oil or ghee at the start of cooking.
- **Ground spices (powders)** — milled spices that blend into gravies, marinades and doughs.

Neither is "better"; they do different jobs. (For a full comparison, see our guide to [whole spices vs ground spices](/blog/spice-guide/whole-spices-vs-ground-spices-which-to-use).)

### The five spices behind most everyday cooking

#### Turmeric (haldi)
Earthy, slightly bitter and deeply yellow, turmeric gives dals, curries and rice their familiar colour. A small amount goes a long way. It is usually added early, once the oil is hot and before liquid goes in, so the raw taste cooks out.

#### Red chilli powder
Chilli brings heat, and different chillies bring different levels of it. Kashmiri chilli is commonly chosen where you want a deep red colour with comparatively mild heat, while a regular red chilli powder is used when you want more heat. Add chilli in stages and taste as you go — it is easier to add than to take away.

#### Coriander powder (dhania)
Mild, citrusy and slightly sweet, ground coriander is the quiet backbone of many gravies. It rounds out sharper spices and helps thicken a sauce slightly.

#### Cumin (jeera)
Warm and earthy. Whole cumin seeds are tempered in hot oil or ghee (they crackle within seconds) to flavour dals and vegetables; ground cumin goes into marinades, gravies and seasoning mixes. Roasting cumin before grinding gives a deeper, nuttier taste.

#### Black pepper (kali mirch)
Sharp, aromatic heat that is different from chilli's burn. Whole peppercorns can go into rice and stock; ground pepper is added near the end for fragrance.

### Other whole spices worth knowing

| Spice | Flavour in one line | Typical use |

| Mustard seeds (sarson/rai) | Pungent, nutty once popped | Tempering for dals, vegetables, pickles |
| Fenugreek seeds (methi) | Bitter-aromatic; use sparingly | Small pinch in tempering, sambar, pickles |
| Fennel seeds (saunf) | Sweet, anise-like | Tempering, meat and vegetable dishes, after-meal |
| Green cardamom (elaichi) | Floral, sweet, cooling aroma | Chai, desserts, rice dishes |
| Cinnamon (dalchini) | Warm, woody, lightly sweet | Biryani, garam masala, desserts |
| Cloves (laung) | Intense, sharp, slightly numbing | Whole in rice and curries; strong, use few |
| Bay leaf (tej patta) | Mild, tea-like fragrance | Rice, dals and gravies |

### From single spices to masalas

A **masala** is a blend. Garam masala, for example, is generally a mix of warm spices such as cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, pepper and others, usually added near the end of cooking for aroma. The exact mix differs from brand to brand and household to household — check the ingredient list on the pack to see what is in your blend. (For example, RUCHI Garam Masala lists small cardamom, cinnamon, black pepper, cloves, mace, nutmeg, bay leaf, star anise, fennel, coriander, green cardamom and big cardamom on its label.)

Other common blends include chaat masala (tangy and savoury), sambar masala (for South Indian lentil stew) and curry powder. Our upcoming guides to understanding Indian masalas and choosing the right masala for each dish go further.

### When to add spices: a simple order

1. **Whole spices into hot oil/ghee first** (cumin, mustard, bay leaf) — wait for the aroma or crackle.
2. **Aromatics** (onion, ginger, garlic) where the recipe uses them.
3. **Ground spices next, briefly** (turmeric, chilli, coriander) — a short fry in oil, then add liquid or tomatoes so they don't scorch.
4. **Finishing spices last** (garam masala, chaat masala, kasoori methi) — their aroma is delicate.

This is a general pattern, not a rule; always follow the specific recipe.

### A starter spice pantry

If you are building a kitchen from scratch, begin with:

- Turmeric powder, red chilli powder, coriander powder, cumin powder
- Whole cumin seeds, mustard seeds, black pepper
- One all-purpose blend (garam masala) and one regional blend that suits how you cook
- Green cardamom and cinnamon sticks for rice, chai and sweets

Add specialities (fenugreek, fennel, cloves, regional blends) as your cooking calls for them.

### Keeping spices fresh

Heat, light, air and moisture all dull a spice. Store them in clean, dry, airtight containers away from sunlight and the heat of the stove, and buy quantities you will use within the best-before period shown on the pack. Our guide on [how to store spices](/blog/spice-guide/how-to-store-spices) covers freshness checks in detail.

### Frequently asked questions

**What are the most common Indian spices?**
Turmeric, red chilli, coriander, cumin and black pepper appear in most everyday recipes, along with mustard seeds, fenugreek, fennel, cardamom, cinnamon and cloves for tempering, rice and sweet dishes.

**Is garam masala the same as curry powder?**
No. Garam masala is typically a blend of warm spices added at the end of cooking, while curry powder is a general-purpose blend used earlier in the pan. Ingredients differ by brand, so check the label.

**Should I buy whole spices or ground spices?**
Both. Whole spices suit tempering and slow cooking; ground spices suit gravies, marinades and quick dishes. Start with the form your favourite recipes ask for.

**How many spices do I really need?**
A dozen well-chosen spices cover most Indian home cooking.
